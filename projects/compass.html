Skylight polarization compass

Investigating how the polarization pattern in skylight can provide a directional reference for navigation.

How skylight encodes direction

Sunlight becomes partially linearly polarized when it scatters off molecules in the atmosphere. In the single-scattering Rayleigh model, the polarization direction is perpendicular to the plane formed by the Sun, the scatterer and the viewing direction. The degree of polarization peaks near a 90° scattering angle in that ideal model. Across an image, those directions form a pattern tied to the Sun’s position.

A rotating linear analyzer turns polarization into an intensity variation. From images taken at different analyzer angles, the linear Stokes components can be estimated. The angle of linear polarization is half of atan2(S₂, S₁), while the degree of linear polarization is √(S₁² + S₂²) / S₀. The angle describes an axis and repeats every 180°, so filtering and error calculations need to handle that wraparound. Clouds, obstructions and weak polarization reduce the reliability of individual pixels.

Research and contribution

I designed a Raspberry Pi camera system with a rotating polarizer and a SolidWorks enclosure. I collected sky-polarization data and investigated exposure, gain and optical filtering so intensity changes could be interpreted in the context of the acquisition settings.

For the analysis, I built a Python linear-regression model for estimating solar azimuth and evaluated error against a simulated Rayleigh-scattering reference. I also explored photoreceptor-inspired filtering, wavelet denoising and sparse coding, and prototyped MATLAB filter stages to study stability, initialization and angle wraparound.
